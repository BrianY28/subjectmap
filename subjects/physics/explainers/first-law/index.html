Newton's first law: inertia

A body keeps its velocity until a net force changes it, and one rule covers a puck on ice, a ball in a braking bus and a spacecraft coasting between the stars.

View in the physics map · Edit this node on GitHub

§ 1 The law

In one plain sentence: a body keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed, or stays at rest, unless a net force acts on it.

Uniform motion means constant velocity: speed and direction both stay fixed. Rest is the special case of zero velocity.

§ 2 Friction dial

A puck slides at 6.00 m/s on a level track. Kinetic friction decelerates it at a = μg, so the net force per kilogram is μg, with g = 9.81 m/s². Set μ to zero and the speed–time graph flattens.

Common mistake: 'Moving things need a force to keep moving.'

Friction and air drag hide the truth: your push usually cancels friction, leaving almost no net force. Remove friction and the puck keeps its velocity with no push at all.

§ 3 Galileo's two ramps

Release a ball from height h on one inclined plane and let it climb a second. With no friction it returns to height h, and a shallower second plane makes d = h / sin θ longer. Galileo argued this from experiments in the Two New Sciences (1638) and made the case for persisting horizontal motion in the Dialogue (1632).

Common mistake: 'Objects naturally slow down.'

Slowing always comes from a force opposing the motion. A puck with μ = 0.10 decelerates at 0.98 m/s², and with μ = 0.00 it does not decelerate at all.

§ 4 Which frame?

A bus brakes at 5.0 m/s² from 4.0 m/s. A ball rests on its frictionless floor, so no horizontal force acts on the ball. Watch the same event from the street and from a seat in the bus.

Ground frame: the ball keeps a constant velocity of 4.0 m/s while the bus slows. Net force on the ball is zero, so its motion stays uniform. The first law holds here.

Common mistake: 'The first law is just F = ma with F = 0.'

In the street frame the ball has zero net force and zero acceleration. In the bus frame it accelerates forward at 5.0 m/s² while nothing pushes it, so F = ma fails until you add a fictitious force −m·abus. The first law identifies the inertial frames where the plain equation works.

§ 5 Where you meet it

  • Seat belts

    A seat belt supplies the force that changes your velocity when a car brakes; without it you keep the car's previous speed.

  • The tablecloth trick

    Pull a cloth quickly and friction acts on the plate for too short a time to change its velocity much.

  • Voyager 1 coasting

    Voyager 1, launched in 1977 and in interstellar space since 2012, moves at about 17 km/s relative to the Sun; the Sun's weak gravity makes its motion nearly but not exactly uniform.

How the idea arrived: Aristotle to Newton
  1. 4th century BCE

    Aristotle held that motion needs a mover; projectiles were kept moving by the air.

  2. 6th century CE

    John Philoponus of Alexandria argued that a thrower impresses a motive force on a projectile.

  3. 14th century

    Jean Buridan made impetus proportional to a body's quantity of matter and to its speed.

  4. 1632 and 1638

    Galileo Galilei: a ball returns to its release height; his inertia was horizontal motion along the Earth's surface.

  5. 1644

    René Descartes, in Principles of Philosophy (1644), first stated straight-line inertia.

  6. 1687

    Isaac Newton made straight-line inertia the first law in the Principia and built mechanics on it.
